Zulu Decoder

Zulu time is the single clock that aviation runs on worldwide, so that a time means the same moment to everyone, everywhere. Convert between Zulu and the four U.S. time zones below, in 24-hour and 12-hour time, with daylight saving handled for you.

What is Zulu time?

Imagine planning a phone call with someone three time zones away. You have to keep asking “your time or mine?” Now imagine doing that at 300 knots, crossing a time zone every hour, with air traffic controllers in several states and a weather report from a fourth.

So aviation gave up on local time. Everyone — pilots, controllers, weather forecasters, the military — agreed to use one clock: the time in Greenwich, England. That is Zulu time. When a pilot in Texas and a controller in New York both say 1800Z, they mean the exact same instant.

Two things make it dependable. It is the same everywhere on Earth, and it never shifts for daylight saving. Your local clock changes twice a year; Zulu never does.

The name is just the alphabet. Pilots spell letters out loud — Alfa, Bravo, Charlie — and the time zone through Greenwich is labelled Z. Spoken aloud, Z is “Zulu.” So 1800Z is read “eighteen hundred Zulu.”

Times are written as four digits: the first two are the hour on a 24-hour clock, the last two the minutes. So 0630Z is 6:30 in the morning in Greenwich, and 1800Z is 6:00 in the evening there.

    Sources

    Zulu and the four-figure clock
    The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) uses Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) for all operations, and the term Zulu may be used to denote it. On the 24-hour clock the hour is the first two figures and the minutes are the last two — which is why the headline reads as four digits and a Z. Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM) 4-2-12, Time Aeronautical Information Publication (AIP) GEN 3.4
    Which zones exist, and where they end
    The Department of Transportation (DOT) oversees standard time. Nine zones were established by the Standard Time Act of 1918 as amended by the Uniform Time Act of 1966, and their geographic limits are written out in Title 49, Part 71 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR). This page covers the four that cover the contiguous states. 49 CFR Part 71, Standard Time Zone Boundaries Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS), history of U.S. time zones
    Daylight saving dates
    Daylight saving time runs from 2 a.m. on the second Sunday in March to 2 a.m. on the first Sunday in November. For 2026 that is 8 March to 1 November. Hawaii, most of Arizona, American Samoa, Guam, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands do not observe it, which is why none of them appear here. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), daylight saving time rules U.S. Naval Observatory, U.S. time zones
    Where the offsets come from
    Every conversion on this page reads your browser's copy of the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA) Time Zone Database, the public-domain record of each zone's offset and daylight saving history. Nothing here is hard-coded, so the page tracks the rules as they are amended. IANA Time Zone Database
    A change now pending
    H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act, would make daylight saving time permanent. The House of Representatives passed it on 14 July 2026 and it is now before the Senate. If it becomes law, the time zone database is updated and this page follows it without any edit. Congress.gov, H.R. 139
