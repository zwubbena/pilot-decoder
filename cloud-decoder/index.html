Background Knowledge

A structured framework for making sense of cloud classification: think of it as a simple matrix built on two axes — altitude (where the cloud lives) and form (what it looks like). Every main cloud type is a combination of an altitude prefix and a form root.

Axis 1 — Form roots (what it looks like)

  • Cumulus (Latin for “heap” or “pile”): puffy, heap-like clouds with distinct outlines and flat bases — floating cotton balls.
  • Stratus (Latin for “layer” or “spread out”): featureless, flat, blanket-like layers covering large areas of sky.
  • Cirrus (Latin for “curl” or “hair”): thin, wispy, fibrous clouds made entirely of ice crystals.
  • Nimbus / nimbo- (Latin for “rain”): a modifier marking a cloud that is actively producing precipitation.

Axis 2 — Altitude tiers (where it lives)

  • High (above ~20,000 ft): prefix Cirro- — Cirrus, Cirrocumulus, Cirrostratus.
  • Middle (~6,500–20,000 ft): prefix Alto- — Altocumulus, Altostratus.
  • Low (surface to ~6,500 ft): no altitude prefix — Stratus, Stratocumulus, Cumulus.

The matrix — the ten genera

Mix an altitude prefix with a form root and the ten fundamental genera fall out:

High (Cirro-)Cirrostratus — high, thin sheetCirrocumulus — high, tiny ripplesCirrus — high, wispy strands—

The exception — vertical development

Cumulonimbus starts low but grows vertically through all three tiers: the classic thunderhead, capable of heavy rain, lightning, hail, and severe turbulence.

*Placed in the low tier here because its base commonly sags low; the WMO International Cloud Atlas formally classes Nimbostratus as a middle-level cloud.
